One thing that many of my academic peers have in common with my family and friends is the confused and regretful look they give me after having asked about the topic of my PhD: *actual causation*.
Isn't it obvious what a cause is?
And anyway, why is this one *actual*?

I start with the question why it's called *actual*: I agree that that's not the clearest word, but everybody in the field is using it.
A better term would be *token causation*. 
Many disciplines distinguish between *types* and *tokens*. 
For example, we can distinguish between the general class "cat", and one specific cat.
The general class would be called a type, and the particular cat is considered a token.
So, in token causation, we are looking at specific instances of causation. 
As an example, compare these two claims: "The Titanic hitting the iceberg caused the Titanic to sink" and "Punching holes in ships causes them to sink". 
The first claim is about two specific events that are not repeatable.
The second claim is a rather general rule about two types of events are usually connected.
Both types of claims can be relevant in different situations: in the particular case of the Titanic sinking, we want to know what caused this particular event.
This is needed to attribute blame and responsibility.
But the more general claim is also useful, especially when thinking about preventing sinking ships in the future.

Now to the second question: isn't it obvious what a cause is? It seems very obvious that the Titanic hitting the iceberg caused the Titanic to sink!
Well, this example might be very clear, but we did only consider two events.
But what if we also consider the weather conditions pushing the iceberg in the way of the ship?
Or the fact that (allegedly) one officer who was not on board of the ship took the key to the locker with the binoculars with him?
The more details we add, the less clear the picture gets.

It is not obvious, and that is why I can do a PhD about it. 
It is true that many people have a relatively clear understanding of this concept, but it is surprisingly difficult to define it accurately.
